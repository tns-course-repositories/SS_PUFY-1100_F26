🧭 Assignment 12 — Framing an Investigation

💡 Introduction

Class 12 — Thursday | 11-12-26 — begins the development of your Sustainable Systems final project. Drawing on the semester’s three connected perspectives—Underland, Energy, and Making—you will identify a question about the material, environmental, or social world and begin shaping a creative investigation.

Your project may take the form of a System (reorganizing relationships, resources, or processes), a Story (revealing or communicating an overlooked condition), a Prototype (testing an alternative material, object, or process), or a combination of these approaches. However: if there is another approach that you would like to defend and advocate, that is more than acceptable, particularly at this early stage.

This assignment is about framing a compelling investigation, not arriving at a finished solution. Work through three exercises that move from individual exploration, to paired questioning, to small-group critique. You remain responsible for developing and submitting your own project investigation throughout.

Process: Generate (individual) → Select (pairs) → Refine (small groups)

🔎 EXERCISE 1 — GENERATE THREE DIRECTIONS

Thursday | 11-12-26 | In class | Individual | Approximately 15 minutes

Begin broadly. Develop three genuinely different final-project directions, rather than three variations of the same idea. You may start with a material, an everyday object, an environmental concern, a place, an overlooked relationship, a production process, or a question raised during the semester.

1 — IDENTIFY A STARTING POINT

What interests you, and why?

For each of your three directions, name a specific topic, material, place, process, or condition. Connect it to at least one of the course perspectives: Underland, Energy, or Making. Avoid broad labels such as “pollution” or “sustainability” without identifying what you would actually investigate.

2 — ASK AN OPEN QUESTION

What do you want to understand, reveal, or change?

Write an initial question for each direction. Focus on a relationship, consequence, contradiction, or possibility that could be investigated. Your question does not need to be perfectly formulated yet, but it should go beyond asking whether something is simply “good” or “bad.”

3 — IMAGINE A CREATIVE APPROACH

How might you investigate or communicate the question?

Identify a possible System, Story, or Prototype approach for each direction. Consider what you might map, document, visualize, reorganize, fabricate, compare, or test. These are tentative methods, not commitments to a final product.

Exercise 1 output: Three short concepts, 2–3 sentences each, identifying a starting point, an initial question, and a possible creative approach.

🗺️ EXERCISE 2 — SELECT + MAP AN INVESTIGATION

Thursday | 11-12-26 | In class | Pairs | Approximately 25 minutes

Work with a partner to test your three directions through conversation. Each student will select and develop their own project. You are choosing the strongest investigation, not necessarily the most polished design idea. Take turns as investigator (presenting ideas) and critical questioner (probing assumptions and possibilities).

1 — PRESENT + QUESTION (ABOUT 12 MINUTES)

Which direction has the most potential—and why?

Each student presents their three directions (about 3 minutes per person). The partner then asks critical questions (about 3 minutes per person) about relevance, originality, research potential, and feasibility. What could realistically be investigated during the remaining project period? Avoid simply voting for a favorite; help your partner uncover a stronger question.

2 — SELECT + FRAME A FOCUSED RESEARCH QUESTION (ABOUT 3 MINUTES)

What, specifically, will your project investigate?

Individually select one direction, write a brief reason for choosing it, and revise your initial question to identify a clear subject and relationship. Consider who or what is involved, where impacts occur, and what remains unknown. A useful question should be focused enough to guide research while remaining open to discovery.

3 — MAP THE SYSTEM (ABOUT 8 MINUTES)

What connects to your chosen issue?

Create a preliminary systems map using nodes, arrows, short labels, and annotations. Include relevant elements such as:

  • Materials + resources: What is extracted, produced, consumed, discarded, or reused?
  • People + institutions: Who makes decisions, performs labor, benefits, or bears consequences?
  • Processes + movement: Where do materials, energy, information, or waste travel?
  • Impacts + tensions: Where do environmental or social consequences, dependencies, or opportunities emerge?

Show relationships, not just a list of things. Use arrows to indicate flows or influences, and identify one important gap or uncertainty that requires further research. The map may be incomplete; its purpose is to expose what you know and what you need to find out.

4 — DEFINE THE PROJECT APPROACH (ABOUT 2 MINUTES)

What might you make, reveal, test, or reorganize?

Choose System, Story, Prototype, or a combination. In 2–3 sentences, explain what form your investigation might take and how that approach would help answer your research question. Distinguish the question you are investigating from the creative outcome you might eventually produce.

Exercise 2 output: Brief notes from the paired exchange; one selected direction with a brief rationale, one revised research question, one labeled preliminary systems map, and a 2–3 sentence approach statement.

🔄 EXERCISE 3 — REFINE THROUGH PEER FEEDBACK

Thursday | 11-12-26 | In class | Small groups of 3–4 | Approximately 20 minutes

Form a group of 3–4 students, with classmates; you can include your Exercise 2 partner. Each student presents an emerging investigation to a broader audience. The goal is to identify what is clear, what is missing, and how the question, system, or creative approach could become more precise. Every student remains responsible for revising their own work.

1 — PRESENT YOUR INVESTIGATION (ABOUT 8 MINUTES)

Can someone else understand what you want to investigate?

Take turns presenting your research question, systems map, and proposed approach (about 2 minutes per student). Explain why the issue matters, what relationships you have identified, and what you still need to learn. Keep presentations focused so every group member can contribute.

2 — GROUP CRITIQUE (ABOUT 7 MINUTES)

What is clear, missing, or assumed?

As a group, discuss the proposals using these prompts. Distribute speaking time so each project receives useful feedback:

  • Focus: Is the research question clear and sufficiently specific? What might be narrowed or clarified?
  • Systems thinking: Which important actor, material, flow, impact, or relationship is missing from the map?
  • Creative approach: Does the proposed System, Story, or Prototype meaningfully investigate the question—or merely illustrate the topic?
  • Feasibility: What is one useful next research step, source, observation, or experiment?
  • Compelling: Does the proposal present an engaging and meaningful investigation that challenges assumptions, raises important questions, or offers a fresh perspective?

As the project author, record at least two substantive comments or questions from group members. You do not need to accept every suggestion, but you should consider what it reveals.

3 — REVISE + IDENTIFY NEXT STEPS (ABOUT 5 MINUTES)

What changes after the conversation?

Revise your research question, systems map, or proposed approach in response to the discussion. Write a brief reflection explaining what you changed and why. Identify two concrete next steps to develop the proposal, such as finding a source, contacting someone, documenting a site, comparing materials, or conducting an initial test.

Exercise 3 output: At least two recorded small-group feedback points, a revised research question or annotated map, a brief explanation of the revision, and two next steps.

🎯 FROM WORKSHOP TO FINAL-PROJECT PROPOSAL

The three exercises form the starting framework for your final project. Your proposal should emerge from an investigation that connects to Underland, Energy, Making, or their intersections—not simply from a preconceived object or aesthetic outcome.

Continue developing your research, systems map, and creative approach after class. The final-project proposal is due Sunday | 11-22-26; follow the separate final-project proposal instructions for its specific requirements.
