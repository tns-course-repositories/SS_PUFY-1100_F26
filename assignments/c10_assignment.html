🧭 Assignment 10 — Biomimicry: Nature as a Design Model

💡 Introduction

Class 10 — Thursday | 10-29-26 — explores how strategies found in living systems can inform new possibilities in art, design, materials, architecture, and everyday life.

Biomimicry begins by looking beyond an object’s appearance to ask what it does and how it works. This two-part investigation moves from an immediate observation in the built environment to an independently researched biological strategy and a proposed design translation.

Process: OBSERVE → INVESTIGATE → ABSTRACT → TRANSLATE

🔎 PART I — IN-CLASS GROUP FIELD INVESTIGATION

Thursday | 10-29-26 | In class

Work in your usual class groups. You will have 20 minutes to explore The UCEN (inside the building) or the immediate block surrounding the building. Look for one object, feature, material, or system that interests you. It may be natural or manufactured: a plant, clothing detail, architectural device, facade, drainage element, street furniture, structural connection, surface, or something else.

There are no restrictions on scale, form, or material. You do not need to find an example of biomimicry already in use. Instead, begin with observation and investigate a function that could lead you toward nature.

1 — OBSERVE + DOCUMENT

What did you find?

Select one example. Record its location and document it with a photograph and a quick sketch. Describe its form, material, construction, or behavior without assuming you already understand it.

2 — IDENTIFY THE FUNCTION

What does it do, or what problem does it address?

Consider functions such as attach, protect, support, shade, cool, cover, filter, transport, absorb, distribute, adapt, or communicate. Identify one primary function and explain what you observe about how it operates.

3 — FIND A BIOLOGICAL PARALLEL

Where might nature accomplish a similar function?

Brainstorm an organism, biological feature, or natural system that addresses the same challenge. The relationship may be speculative at this stage; distinguish a plausible analogy from a verified biological mechanism.

4 — ABSTRACT THE STRATEGY

What is the underlying principle?

Make a simple functional diagram, using arrows, labels, or sectional sketches. Focus on the mechanism, not just visual resemblance. Describe the principle in one sentence.

5 — TRANSLATE INTO A NEW APPLICATION

How might this principle inform art or design?

Sketch a possible application in an artwork, material, object, building, or system. It need not be fully resolved or technically proven. Identify one question you would need to investigate further.

6 — GROUP DISCUSSION

What did your group discover?

Share your observation, identified function, proposed biological parallel, and design sketch with the class. Present the sequence OBSERVED OBJECT → FUNCTION → NATURE → NEW APPLICATION.

📝 PART II — INDEPENDENT BIOMIMICRY INVESTIGATION

Complete individually outside of class.

Use the same investigation process, but you are not limited to UCEN, the neighborhood, or physical fieldwork. You may begin with a natural organism, a design problem, a work of art, a material behavior, a building, a scientific discovery, or an existing technology. Use observation, books, articles, image archives, videos, or research databases to develop your investigation.

Starting prompts — Choose your own point of entry (examples below)

  • Begin with a problem: How might we attach without glue, cool without conventional air-conditioning, or build with less material?
  • Begin with an organism: How does a leaf shed water, a shell resist force, a spider construct a web, or a seed travel?
  • Begin with a material or surface: What biological strategy could inform its flexibility, strength, texture, responsiveness, or fabrication?
  • Begin with a place or structure: How might a facade, shelter, pathway, or public space behave more like a living system?
  • Begin with art making: Could a biological strategy change how an artwork is assembled, supported, transformed, or experienced?
  • Begin with an existing invention: What natural strategy inspired it, and how could that strategy be translated differently?

1 — OBSERVE + SELECT

Identify your starting point and explain why you selected it. Include an image or sketch that makes the example understandable.

2 — IDENTIFY THE FUNCTION

Define the central problem or function you want to explore. State it in functional language rather than describing appearance alone.

3 — INVESTIGATE THE BIOLOGY

Research one specific organism, biological structure, process, or ecosystem strategy that addresses this function. Explain how it works, using credible sources. Distinguish established mechanisms from your own interpretations.

4 — ABSTRACT THE STRATEGY

Produce a labeled diagram showing the functional principle. What relationships, movements, structures, or material properties are essential? What can be removed without losing the mechanism?

5 — TRANSLATE INTO ART OR DESIGN

Propose an original application, using a sketch, collage, diagram, or other visual study. Explain how the biological principle informs the proposal, rather than simply copying the organism’s appearance.

6 — EVALUATE + REFLECT

What is your original contribution? Identify how your proposal meaningfully transforms, extends, or recontextualizes the biological strategy beyond an existing example. A new application or significant reinterpretation is sufficient; you do not need to invent an entirely new mechanism.

What might the translation make possible? Consider material use, energy, adaptability, durability, environmental impact, or another relevant measure. Explain at least one limitation or unanswered question. A nature-inspired design is not automatically sustainable; any claimed benefits need evidence or further testing.

📚 OPTIONAL REFERENCE — DESIGN PARADIGMS SOURCEBOOK

Design Paradigms Sourcebook

Use this sourcebook for inspiration about structural, functional, and organizational principles. Its examples can help you recognize patterns, develop questions, and consider possible design strategies.

Reference is not reproduction. You may begin with a paradigm from the sourcebook, but your proposal must make a meaningful original contribution: a new application, significant transformation, or substantially different design strategy. Simply copying an illustrated example or changing its appearance is not sufficient.

If you use the sourcebook, identify the reference, explain the principle you borrowed, and show what your translation changes or adds. Not every paradigm in the sourcebook is biological; your investigation must still research and explain a specific biological strategy.
