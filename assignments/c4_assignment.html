🧭 Assignment 4 - Mapping Uneven Systems

💡 Introduction

Environmental justice is spatial. Environmental benefits and burdens are distributed unevenly across communities, territories, and scales. But those relationships are not always obvious from a map, an aerial image, or a statistical designation alone.

Throughout Class 4, we examined externalities, intensification, proximity, and environmental justice as ways of understanding how environmental costs are distributed. We also established an important distinction:

Proximity ≠ distance.

Straight-line distance can help describe a relationship between an environmental justice site and a community, but exposure can also move through air, water, infrastructure, movement, and time.

This assignment asks you to move between data → site → aerial imagery → physical removal in order to investigate that relationship.

The assignment has two parts:

PART I — EXPLORE (In Class)
Use New York State environmental justice data and aerial imagery to compare statistical designation with the actual built environment.

PART II — INVESTIGATE + REMOVE (Outside Class)
Select one operating environmental justice conflict from the EJAtlas, locate it independently in GeoLibre, investigate its spatial relationship to surrounding communities, and print an 11 × 17 inch aerial image. Using the project boundary as precisely as possible, physically remove the EJ site from the image, retaining both the positive — the removed project site — and the negative — the surrounding aerial image containing the resulting void — for the Class 5 pin-up.

Removing the project site separates the source of an environmental conflict from the territory in which its impacts are experienced. By retaining both the positive and negative pieces, the Class 5 pin-up allows us to compare how different sites relate to their surrounding communities — and how environmental impacts may extend beyond a project’s physical boundary.

The completed physical work will return to class for a Class 5 pin-up and comparison exercise.

🛰️️ Visual Precedent · Jenny Odell

Satellite Landscapes — Jenny Odell

Jenny Odell’s Satellite Landscapes isolates pieces of infrastructure from satellite imagery, removing them from their original geographic context and turning ordinarily embedded systems into discrete visual objects.

We borrow Odell’s strategy of extraction as a way of seeing. By physically separating the EJ project from its surrounding landscape, we make both the project and the context it occupied newly visible.

Unlike Odell, however, you will retain both sides of the extraction:

  • POSITIVE — SITE: the EJ project removed from the aerial image and mounted separately on white paper.
  • NEGATIVE — TERRITORY: the original 11 × 17 inch aerial image containing the project-shaped void and its surrounding communities, infrastructure, and landscape.

Removing the project site temporarily separates the source of an environmental conflict from the territory in which its impacts are experienced. During the Class 5 pin-up, we will bring these paired objects back into relationship and compare how different project sites relate to their surrounding communities—and how environmental impacts may extend beyond a project’s physical boundary.

✂️ PART II — OUTSIDE CLASS

FIND → RESEARCH → LOCATE → FRAME → MEASURE → PRINT → REMOVE → OBSERVE

Part II asks you to apply the Class 4 spatial framework to one operating environmental justice site.

01 — FIND AN EJ SITE

Open the Environmental Justice Atlas — EJAtlas.

Use the project-status filter:

Project Status → In operation

Your selected project must currently be in operation.

You may begin your search using:

  • the place where you grew up
  • a place where you have lived
  • a place with which you strongly identify
  • another location that gives you a meaningful geographic starting point

The goal is to use personal geographic familiarity as a search filter, not as the subject of the assignment.

Choose a project for which you can reasonably identify the physical project site in aerial imagery.

Important

EJAtlas is your research and discovery source. It is not your final mapping environment.

Once you identify your project, you must independently locate and examine the site in GeoLibre using Esri aerial imagery.

02 — RESEARCH THE SITE

Use the EJAtlas entry and its cited/contextual information to understand the conflict before mapping it.

Record the following:

  • Project / conflict name
  • Location
  • Project type / industry
  • Current project status
  • Primary environmental justice issue
  • Community or communities identified as affected
  • Environmental burden(s) or concern(s) described by the source

Use the lecture lenses to begin interpreting the site:

INTENSIFICATION — Are multiple burdens, facilities, infrastructures, or histories concentrated here?

PROXIMITY — How is the project spatially related to surrounding communities?

TIME — Is the issue acute, chronic, cumulative, or associated with long-term exposure?

Do not invent an exposure pathway or impact that your research does not support.

03 — LOCATE THE SITE IN GEOLIBRE

Determine the project location as precisely as possible.

The EJAtlas map marker and contextual information can help you orient yourself. If available, identify an approximate latitude / longitude for the center of the project site and use those coordinates to navigate in GeoLibre.

Then:

  1. Open GeoLibre.
  2. Turn on the Esri aerial imagery basemap.
  3. Navigate to the project location.
  4. Use visible site features and your research to identify the project boundary as accurately as possible.
  5. Do not use the EJAtlas map itself as your final map.

04 — FRAME THE AERIAL IMAGE

Your final print will be 11 × 17 inches and may be either:

  • Landscape, or
  • Portrait.

There is no required common map scale for the class.

Instead, use the following framing rule:

The EJ project site should occupy approximately 1/3 of the total paper area.

Adjust your GeoLibre scale until:

  • the project site is clearly identifiable
  • enough surrounding territory remains visible to understand its context
  • nearby communities, occupied areas, infrastructure, or other relevant spatial relationships can be seen where possible

Different projects will therefore use different map scales. The consistent element across the class is the 11 × 17 format + 1/3 site-occupation rule.

05 — MEASURE STRAIGHT-LINE DISTANCE

Before exporting your map, use a measurement tool to estimate the straight-line distance between the EJ project site and one relevant nearby community or occupied area identified through your research and aerial investigation.

Record the approximate distance in an appropriate unit:

  • feet / miles, or
  • meters / kilometers

This measurement is descriptive, not a complete measure of exposure.

Remember:

Proximity ≠ distance.

Use either method to estimate the straight-line distance between the EJ project and a nearby potentially impacted community:

METHOD 1 · Controls → Measure
  • Open Controls → Measure.
  • Select the distance / line measurement tool.
  • Click once at the edge of the EJ project site.
  • Click at the nearest relevant community or residential area.
  • Record the approximate straight-line distance for your Site Observation Detail Sheet.
METHOD 2 · Plugins → Dimensions
  • Open Plugins and activate Dimensions.
  • Select the Dimensions / distance tool once it appears in the interface.
  • Draw a dimension from the edge of the EJ project site to the relevant community.
  • Read and record the displayed distance.
  • Keep the dimension visible while examining the aerial imagery if it helps you understand the site’s spatial relationship to its surroundings.

Remember:

This measurement describes straight-line distance only. It does not by itself establish exposure or impact. Use your research to consider whether air, water, infrastructure, movement, accumulation, or other pathways create a different relationship between the project and surrounding communities.

08 — REMOVE THE EJ SITE

Bring your printed 11 × 17 aerial image to a safe cutting surface.

Using the razor blade and cardboard cutting backing provided in class:

  1. Carefully identify the visible boundary of the EJ project site.
  2. Cut along that boundary as accurately as the aerial image and your research allow.
  3. Remove the complete project site from the 11 × 17 aerial image.
  4. Keep both pieces.

You will now have:

A — THE SITE
The removed EJ project footprint.

B — THE CONTEXT
The remaining 11 × 17 aerial image containing the surrounding territory and a project-shaped void.

Important

Do not discard either piece. Both are required for the Class 5 pin-up.

09 — MOUNT THE REMOVED SITE

Mount the removed EJ project piece on plain white paper.

Bristol paper is recommended because it is clean, rigid, and appropriate for pin-up presentation.

You are responsible for obtaining the white mounting paper.

Keep the mounting simple. The objective is to isolate the project’s physical footprint, not to decorate or redesign it.

Bring both physical components to Class 5:

  1. Mounted EJ project removal
  2. Remaining 11 × 17 aerial context / void

Together, these two objects will become the basis of our comparative pin-up.
