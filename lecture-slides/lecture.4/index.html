Energy is Uneven

  • Energy consumption is highly uneven.

  • High-energy lifestyles depend on high energy flow.

  • Consumption Geographies ≠ Production Geographies.

  • Benefit Geographies ≠ Burdened Geographies.

Source: Our World In Data

Every System Produces Payoffs

  • Energy systems produce useful outcomes.
  • Energy enables mobility, production, heating, cooling, and communication.
  • Economic and social value.
  • But we must ask: what value exactly — and for whom.

Source: Nasa Worldview - Night Lights

Payoffs Require Tradoffs

  • Systems operate within constraints and competing priorities.
  • Increasing one benefit can require giving something else up.
  • Tradeoffs distribute gains and losses differently.
  • Ask: What is gained? What is lost? Who decides?

Who Receives Benefits?

  • Extraction and production generate goods, energy, jobs, and profits.

  • Global systems move these benefits across enormous distances.

  • Consumption can occur far from extraction and production.

Who Bears the Costs?

Who Bears the Costs?

  • Extraction and production also generate waste, pollution, risk, and disruption.
  • These costs are often concentrated near sites of extraction, production, and disposal.
  • The people receiving the benefits may be different from those experiencing the burdens.
  • Ask: Who bears the cost—and where are they?

Externality

Negative Externality

  • A decision can create a private benefit while imposing costs on others.

  • Driving provides mobility, convenience, and access to the driver.

  • Traffic produces air pollution, congestion, noise, and risk beyond the driver.

  • These additional costs are external to the individual transaction.

Capitalism is the System

Capitalism is an economic system in which productive assets are largely privately owned, goods and services are exchanged through markets, and production is generally organized in pursuit of profit.

  • Private ownership — individuals and firms control productive assets.

  • Markets — goods, services, labor, and resources are exchanged.

  • Profit — production seeks returns beyond its costs.

  • Competition — creates pressure for cost reduction.

Costs have Geography

Elsewhere is a place.

  • Environmental costs are distributed across people, places, and time.

  • Production and consumption often occur in different geographies.

  • Infrastructure connects distant places into a single system.

  • Externalities can therefore extend far beyond the site of production.

Systems Connect Places

  • Materials and energy move through linked stages of production and consumption.

  • Extraction, processing, transport, use, and disposal occur in different places.

  • Benefits can move through the system while costs remain concentrated at particular sites.

  • A local activity can therefore belong to a much larger territorial system.

Burdens Accumulate (Intensification)

  • Externalities rarely occur one at a time.
  • Infrastructure and industry tend to cluster in particular places.
  • Multiple sources can produce overlapping environmental burdens.
  • Burdens can also accumulate over time and across generations.

Proximity Matters

  • Environmental risk is often understood through distance from a source.

  • Greater proximity can mean greater exposure, vulnerability, or consequence.

  • Boundaries and buffer zones attempt to translate risk into measurable space.

  • But distance alone does not determine impact.

Proximity ≠ Distance

  • Distance measures how far one place is from another.

  • Proximity describes how places are connected through environmental and infrastructural systems.

  • Air, water, infrastructure, and movement can extend impacts beyond physical adjacency.

  • Proximity is therefore a relationship, not simply a measurement.

Fixed ‘in place’ Vulnerability - Environmental Justice (EJ)

Environmental justice asks whether environmental benefits, burdens, and decision-making power are distributed fairly.

  • Environmental conditions are shaped by social, economic, and political systems.

  • Communities do not have equal exposure to environmental benefits and burdens.

  • Communities also differ in their ability to influence environmental decisions.

1982 Warren County, North Carolina PCB landfill protests

Environmental justice isn’t simply the study of environmental inequality. It emerged as a demand by communities for fair treatment and meaningful participation in decisions affecting their environments.

Disproportionate Burden

  • Environmental hazards and amenities are not distributed equally.

  • Low-income communities and communities of color have often experienced greater environmental burdens.

  • These patterns reflect histories of land use, housing, infrastructure, industry, and political power.

  • EJ asks whether unequal exposure becomes disproportionate burden.

The Proximity of Vulnerable Populations:

  • A fenceline community or frontline community is a neighborhood that is immediately adjacent to a company, military base, industrial or service center and is directly affected by the noise, odors, chemical emissions, traffic, parking, or operations of the company.

  • These communities are exposed to hazardous chemicals, high pollution levels, and environmental degradation along with the threat of chemical explosions.

  • Many fenceline communities are situated in sacrifice zones that are disproportionately inhabited by people of color, Indigenous communities, and the working poor.

Source: Fenceline community

4 Important Questions to ask when Confronting Environmental Justice Issues:

  1. What is the ‘Size’ or ‘Scale’ of both the hazard (environmental violence) and the vulnerable population(s)?

  2. What is the ‘Speed’ of the hazard (environmental violence) - Fast or Slow?

  3. How is a vulnerable population(s) typified? What is the regime of measurement for a vulnerable population(s)?

  4. How is the hazard (environmental violence) measured? How does this measurement relate to its regulatory framework?
